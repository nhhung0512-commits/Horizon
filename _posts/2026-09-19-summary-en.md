## [Terry Tao: Math Is More Than Proof, So Celebrate the Rest](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/18/if-math-is-more-than-proof-we-need-to-better-celebrate-the-rest-of-it/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Terence Tao published an essay on his blog on September 18, 2026 titled "If math is more than proof, we need to better celebrate the rest of it," arguing that the discipline's intuitive, exploratory and communicative dimensions deserve far more recognition than they currently receive. The post quickly became a widely discussed piece, drawing hundreds of comments focused on how AI is reshaping what mathematical work actually is. The essay reframes a cultural question — which parts of mathematical practice count as real mathematics — at a moment when AI systems are increasingly capable of producing machine-checkable proofs, threatening to reduce the human role to proof generation. If the field accepts Tao's framing, intuition, problem selection, exposition and pedagogy could become recognized research contributions rather than invisible supporting work. The piece is a reflective blog essay rather than a technical result or announcement, so it contains no new theorems or benchmarks; its influence comes from Tao's standing and the timing of the AI debate. The discussion it triggered on Hacker News reached roughly 286 points and 228 comments, spanning mathematician job models, formal verification, and the writing genre known as "discovery fiction."

**Background**: Terence Tao is a Fields Medalist (2006) widely regarded as one of the most influential living mathematicians, and his blog is a long-standing venue for methodological reflection read well beyond his own subfield. A central subplot of the debate is automated theorem proving — the subfield of automated reasoning in which computer programs search for and check mathematical proofs — which has advanced rapidly, though current systems still struggle to judge which theorems and proofs are interesting rather than trivial.

**Discussion**: Commenters largely agreed that proof-centric culture has crowded out intuition, invoking the 1900 Poincaré–Hilbert debate and lamenting how school and applied mathematics lost that intuitive side, while one reader recommended Michael Nielsen's "discovery fiction" as a model for exposing rigor through narrative. A sharper thread argued that mathematicians now face a more violent version of what programmers have weathered, since task-level work like proving hard lemmas *was* the job that earned tenure, and one commenter noted that the Fields Medal's age limit reflects a bias toward raw brainpower over deep understanding.
